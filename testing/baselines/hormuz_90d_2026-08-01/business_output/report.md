## Snapshot

The Strait of Hormuz has been under active war conditions since 28 February 2026, and as of late July transit volumes have collapsed to roughly 39 crossings in a week (versus an 82-transit prior week and an ~88/day historical baseline), with single-day counts as low as 10 [s02, s04]. IRGC struck and detained tankers again on 29 and 31 July, including vessels under US military air escort, while CENTCOM has conducted sustained retaliatory strikes on Iranian coastal military infrastructure [s01, s09, s19]. This is a materially more severe and more durable disruption than the "elevated cost, intact throughput" framing in the working thesis suggests.

## Evidence highlights

- **Transit volumes have already collapsed, not just dipped.** Weekly transits fell from ~109 two weeks prior to 85 to 39 by 13-20 July, with a single-day low of 10 versus an ~88/day baseline; non-Iranian-linked traffic fell 27% week-on-week [s02, s04].

- **War-risk insurance has repriced sharply but has not been formally withdrawn.** Hull war-risk premiums rose from ~1-3% to 7.5-10% of hull value in July; underwriters are reluctant on spot terms, but aggregate global war-risk capacity ($2.5-3bn) still exceeds the exposure of individual Gulf-transiting tankers [s05, s11, s13].

- **Bypass capacity is real but small relative to what normally transits.** The Saudi East-West pipeline runs at its 7mn b/d ceiling, but Yanbu's loading terminals cap out near 4.5mn b/d and were still exporting only ~1.1mn b/d in February — near their historical baseline, not surged. ADNOC's Habshan-Fujairah line is capped at 1.5mn b/d. Combined, bypass capacity is a few mb/d against the ~20mb/d that normally transits the strait [s12, s25, s26].

- **OPEC+'s nominal spare capacity is not currently a deliverable offset.** Quotas have been raised four times since the crisis began, but Gulf-member production/exports have fallen (33.19mn b/d in April vs 42.77mn in February per OPEC figures) because the barrels sit geographically behind the strait [s15, s22].

- **The IEA's headline emergency-response tool has already been spent, not merely readied.** A record 400mn-barrel coordinated release was executed in March 2026, the largest in IEA history — this lever is not sitting in reserve for a fresh shock [s07].

- **China-bound Iranian flows are being squeezed by both sanctions enforcement and Iran's own export collapse.** Treasury has sanctioned Shandong teapot refineries buying Iranian crude; independently, Kharg loadings fell from ~1.4mn b/d in February to ~0.32mn b/d by mid-April [s10, s16, s17].

## How news reshapes the working thesis

The working thesis's central claim — that price impact is being driven more by freight/insurance cost inflation and precautionary self-diversion than an outright volume shock, with throughput "intact but pricier" — is weakened by the transit data itself: volumes are down 55-89% depending on the window measured, which is a volume shock, not a cost-inflation story layered on intact flow [s02, s04]. The scenario set's "managed escalation, no formal closure" base case (s1, originally 50%) no longer matches observed reality, where IRGC has declared and enforced closure repeatedly since February with two failed ceasefires; the evidence instead tracks closer to the plan's own "attempted blockade or major disruption" scenario (s3), which should be reweighted up materially. The long-Brent and long-refining-margin legs of the book are consistent with what's observed; the short-freight-optionality leg is the most exposed, given VLCC rate spikes and hull premiums that are already realized volatility, not tail risk.

## Risks & blind spots

- Several key data points (transit counts, Kharg lifting volumes, insurance capacity) come from tracking services and trade press rather than primary AIS/customs data directly queried by this run; official Iranian (PMO, SHANA) and Saudi (Mawani) sites returned only generic background pages, not current operational data, despite being queried directly [q03, q04, q07, q08 in executed_queries].
- "Day count since 28 February" framing from independent trackers (straits.live) is not independently verified against an official closure declaration and should be treated as directionally, not precisely, accurate.
- No confirmation was found in this run of Saudi or UAE actually surging bypass throughput above baseline; absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but it argues against assuming rerouting is already happening at scale.
- Diplomatic track (Iran-Oman technical talks referenced by Iran's MFA) is a live but thin signal on de-escalation odds; it was not corroborated by a second independent source in this run.

## Open questions

- Has any of the OPEC+ nominal quota increase actually reached the market, or does it remain trapped behind the strait?
- What is the current (post-July) NIOC/Kharg export run-rate, and is Iran approaching hard storage constraints that could force upstream shut-ins?
- At what loss level would marine war-risk underwriters move from higher pricing to outright non-renewal of Gulf cover?
- Could further strikes target undamaged Ras Laffan trains, or the Fujairah/Yanbu bypass infrastructure itself?
- Does the Iran-Oman technical talks channel have any realistic probability of producing a durable ceasefire, distinct from the two ceasefires that have already failed (8 April, and the now-void Islamabad MOU)?
- How exposed is the refining-margin leg of the book to Gulf refiners' own feedstock and logistics disruption, versus benefiting purely from a widening Brent-product spread?
